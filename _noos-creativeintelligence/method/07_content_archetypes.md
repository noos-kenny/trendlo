# 07 — Content Archetypes

Six recurring formats we use to structure creative production for fashion and adjacent retail. Each archetype is a *container* — it has a default shape, a primary trigger, and a set of production conventions. The point of archetypes is to make production scalable without flattening into sameness.

Archetypes are not categories of *idea* — they are categories of *frame*. The same idea can be expressed in three archetypes; the same archetype can carry many ideas.

## The six archetypes at a glance

| # | Archetype | Primary trigger | Funnel stage | Production weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Community Portrait | Similarity Proof / Identity Signaling | Prospecting → MOF | Medium |
| 2 | Cultural Storytelling | Identity Signaling / Cultural Authority | Prospecting | High |
| 3 | Product Ritual | Aspiration / Transformation Proof | Prospecting → MOF | Medium |
| 4 | Craft & Detail | Authority / Transparency | MOF | Medium-high |
| 5 | Drop Narrative | Scarcity / Loss Aversion | Prospecting → BOF (campaign-bound) | Medium |
| 6 | Transformation | Transformation Proof / Curiosity | MOF → BOF | Low-medium |

A healthy fashion account runs all six over a quarter, with weighting tuned to the brand and stage of growth.

## 1. Community Portrait

**What it is.** A documentary-style portrait of a real customer or community member in their own life. Often 30–90s video; also strong as still photography series.

**Primary trigger.** Similarity Proof, frequently layered with Identity Signaling.

**Why it works.** The viewer sees someone like themselves *living with* the product without performing for camera. The proof is in the mundanity — the product is part of a life, not posed against one.

**Production conventions.**

- Subject is a real customer or close to it (micro-tastemaker, named in titles).
- Single location, often a home or workspace.
- Voiceover from the subject in their own words, lightly edited.
- Product is *worn or used* in context, never explicitly held up.
- Shot in natural light where possible; if studio, studio is *not* the read.

**Common mistakes.**

- Casting models who read as models.
- Over-stylising the location until it stops feeling lived-in.
- Letting the brand voice over-write the subject's voice.

**Best paired with.** Quiz-driven retargeting, founder explainer at MOF, drop announcement at BOF.

## 2. Cultural Storytelling

**What it is.** Brand-world content that establishes the cultural context the brand belongs to. Heritage films, archival content, designer profiles, place-based films, mood films tied to a collection.

**Primary trigger.** Identity Signaling, Cultural Authority.

**Why it works.** Fashion is consumed culturally. The audience is buying a place in a story; the storytelling assets *are* that place. This is upper-funnel brand-equity work that compounds over years.

**Production conventions.**

- Higher production value, longer-form.
- Editorial pacing — slower than performance creative.
- Often no direct CTA. Brand search lift and audience build are the goals.
- Music, voiceover, and grading all carry information.

**Common mistakes.**

- Over-investing here while neglecting performance creative — beautiful brand films do not, alone, hit ROAS.
- Storytelling without a *specific* cultural anchor — generic "lifestyle" cinematography is interchangeable with every other brand's lifestyle cinematography.

**Best paired with.** Always run alongside performance creative, not instead of it. Use as the prospecting top-of-funnel that warms cold audiences into the rest of the system.

## 3. Product Ritual

**What it is.** Content showing how the product is used in a specific repeating moment of life — the morning coffee, the after-work change, the weekend trip pack, the dinner-party set.

**Primary trigger.** Aspiration; secondary Transformation Proof.

**Why it works.** It renders the product as part of a life-pattern the viewer wants — and shows them exactly where it fits. The aspiration is rendered concrete: not "imagine the life you want," but "this is what 8 a.m. looks like with this in it."

**Production conventions.**

- Single ritual per asset. Don't try to show "all the ways."
- Short formats (15–30s) work well; longer formats can be paced as a slow montage.
- The product is present but not over-emphasised.
- Time-of-day cues (light, sound, sequence) carry the ritual frame.

**Common mistakes.**

- Forcing rituals the brand has no honest claim to.
- Showing too many products, breaking the singular-moment frame.
- Overproducing — the ritual feels honest at slight imperfection.

**Best paired with.** Continuity media weight on prospecting and MOF audiences.

## 4. Craft & Detail

**What it is.** Content that shows how the product is made, what it is made of, who makes it, and why it is built the way it is. Founder explanations, atelier footage, fabric tests, archive deep-dives.

**Primary trigger.** Authority Framing, Transparency.

**Why it works.** Premium pricing requires the buyer to believe the product is worth more than it costs to produce. Craft & Detail content shows the answer.

**Production conventions.**

- Often founder- or maker-led on camera.
- Functional, not stylised. Workshop, factory, mill.
- One detail per asset — the gram weight, the seam, the dye, the fastening.
- Long-form acceptable here; the engaged audience will watch.

**Common mistakes.**

- Vague "behind the scenes" content with no specific claim.
- Overproduced "atelier" sequences that look staged.
- Authority sources the audience does not recognise (use Nordic-relevant references for Nordic audiences).

**Best paired with.** MOF audiences, retargeting from product page views, abandoned-cart sequences.

## 5. Drop Narrative

**What it is.** Campaign-bound content tied to a launch, drop, or limited release. Pre-announce, launch, in-window, and (cautiously) post-window assets.

**Primary trigger.** Scarcity / Loss Aversion; layered with Identity Signaling and Aspiration in pre-announce, Volume Social Proof in post-launch.

**Why it works.** Drops are honest scarcity. The audience accepts urgency framing because the urgency is real. The trigger is potent here in a way it cannot be elsewhere without damage.

**Production conventions.**

- A clear *visual identity for the drop* — colourway, type, mood — distinct from continuity creative.
- Pre-announce: tease, build the list, layer Commitment & Consistency ("be first").
- Launch: convert; lead with availability and clear access ("Available now at noos.se").
- In-window: layer Volume Social Proof as it accumulates ("70% sold through in 24 hours").
- Post-window: discipline. Either close cleanly or open a wait-list — *not* "last chance" emails after the drop has technically sold out.

**Common mistakes.**

- Recycling drop creative onto continuity products afterwards.
- Manufactured urgency that quietly extends the window.
- Failing to brief the rest of the funnel — drops should pull the whole funnel into a campaign rhythm, not just sit on top of it.

**Best paired with.** A pre-launch list (Commitment & Consistency mechanic), and post-launch continuity creative that picks up the audience built during the drop.

## 6. Transformation

**What it is.** Before / after, week 1 / week 8, outfit change, room change. Visualised change as proof.

**Primary trigger.** Transformation Proof; secondary Curiosity (in the hook).

**Why it works.** Concrete visual change is more persuasive than abstract claims. The buyer sees the forecast.

**Production conventions.**

- Identical lighting, framing, and crop in before/after.
- Honest representation — nothing exaggerated by retouch beyond what the buyer would experience.
- Time-stamped if applicable.
- For fashion, *outfit* transformations work better than *body* transformations (the latter risks identity issues and may run afoul of platform policies).

**Common mistakes.**

- Stylised "afters" that look unattainable.
- Implausible time-frames.
- Body-shaming framings — never acceptable, and increasingly enforced by platforms.

**Best paired with.** UGC programmes, MOF audiences, BOF retargeting on warm audiences.

## EGC — the expert / spokesperson modality

**Employee-Generated Content is not a seventh archetype — it is a casting and sourcing modality** that can carry several of the six, most naturally Craft & Detail, Product Ritual, and Cultural Storytelling. The spokesperson is an employee of the brand (in-house expert, craftsperson, store staff, founder), which activates Authority + Social Proof structurally differently from creator content or customer UGC: scalable over time, brand-controlled, cost-fixed, lower risk. See the framing-modifier section in `02_psychological_trigger_taxonomy.md`.

When auditing or briefing, tag EGC explicitly — it is routinely the largest untapped format opportunity in expertise-led categories (fragrance, wine, hi-fi, skincare, finance, optics), especially where strong EGC exists in organic but 0 % in paid.

## How to brief an archetype

A creative brief at NOOS includes:

1. **Archetype** — which one, and why.
2. **Primary trigger** — must be one of the 12.
3. **Audience and stage** — pulled from the hypothesis.
4. **Anchor** — the single specific element the asset will hang on (the customer's apartment, the gram weight of the denim, the morning coffee ritual, the colour of this drop, the seam detail).
5. **Brand-voice constraints** — the tonal guard-rails.
6. **Deliverables** — formats, durations, ratios.

A brief that names the archetype but cannot name the anchor produces interchangeable content. The anchor is what makes the asset specific to *this* brand at *this* moment.

## Production density and rotation

Recommended rotation for a mid-sized fashion account, monthly:

- 2–3 Community Portraits
- 1 Cultural Storytelling piece (or 1 every two months at higher production)
- 3–4 Product Rituals
- 1–2 Craft & Detail pieces
- Drop Narrative on campaign cadence (quarterly typical)
- 2–3 Transformation pieces (often UGC-mined)

This is a portfolio, not a checklist. Tune to the brand's stage, calendar, and budget — but a quarter without all six archetypes is a sign of an unbalanced creative system.
