# 02 — Psychological Trigger Taxonomy

The taxonomy is the operational vocabulary of the system. Fifteen triggers, organised in five clusters by the *job* they do for the buyer's mind.

This is the canonical taxonomy (decided by Kenny 2026-08-14): **15 triggers in 5 clusters**, matching `04_15_triggers_library.md` in the NOOS creative methodology library. Every trigger list in every NOOS source — skills, picklists, client deliverables — derives from this.

A trigger is not an idea or a creative format. It is the **psychological mechanism** the asset is engineered to activate. The same mechanism can be expressed in many ways — what unifies them is the underlying job: reduce risk, signal identity, shortcut evaluation, capture attention.

Triggers are **vertical-agnostic at the level of mechanism**. The same trigger drives behaviour in e-commerce, lead generation, and B2B SaaS — what changes is the *expression* and the *weighting*. See `06_vertical_adaptations.md` for vertical-specific weighting.

## Cluster overview

| Cluster | Job for the buyer | Triggers |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Risk & Trust Reduction | Lower the perceived cost of being wrong | Risk Reversal, Authority Framing, Transparency |
| 2. Motivation & Identity | Make the buy feel meaningful | Identity Signaling, Aspiration, Commitment & Consistency |
| 3. Social & Proof Mechanics | Borrow conviction from others | Volume Social Proof, Similarity Proof, Transformation Proof |
| 4. Attention & Cognitive Bias | Win the first 1.5 seconds | Loss Aversion, Curiosity / Open Loop, Contrast & Anchoring |
| 5. Urgency & Value | Make acting now rational | Scarcity / Urgency, Reciprocity, Endowment Effect |

The clusters are not mutually exclusive. A single ad can layer two or three triggers — but should always have a *primary* one that the hypothesis is built around. If you cannot name the primary trigger in one word, the asset is muddled.

## Cluster 1 — Risk & Trust Reduction

**The job:** make the *cost of being wrong* feel small enough that the customer can say yes.

This cluster matters most where the perceived risk of a bad purchase or commitment is high: higher price points, repeat-purchase products with switching cost, multi-stakeholder B2B decisions, and any category where the buyer has been burned before.

### 1. Risk Reversal

**Mechanism.** Move risk from the buyer to the seller. The asset makes the cost of saying yes-and-being-wrong smaller than the cost of saying no-and-missing-out.

**Academic grounding.** Loss Aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) — losing money on a bad purchase hurts more than the equivalent saving from a good deal. Risk Reversal directly cancels the asymmetry. Also relates to *Endowment Effect* (Thaler, 1980): once a customer has the product in hand, they are reluctant to return it, so generous return policies cost less than expected.

**When it works hardest.**
- BOF / conversion stage.
- Considered purchases — categories where the buyer hesitates before committing.
- First-time buyers from cold paid traffic.
- B2B SaaS demos, free trials, money-back guarantees on annual contracts.
- Lead-gen offers where the perceived cost is "time wasted on a sales call."

**Expression by vertical.**
- **E-commerce.** Free returns, satisfaction guarantees, "try it for 30 days," "we'll buy it back" offers.
- **D2C subscription.** Pause anytime, skip a delivery, money-back on the first month.
- **B2B SaaS / lead gen.** Free trial, no credit card, money-back guarantee, "30-min meeting, no commitment."
- **B2C lead gen.** "No obligation quote," "we'll only call if you want us to."

**When it underperforms.**
- Heavily commodified categories where buyers expect risk reversal as table stakes — the trigger no longer differentiates.
- Premium positioning, where over-prominent guarantees can erode prestige cues.
- Already-warm B2B audiences who view risk reversal as a sign of vendor desperation.

**Creative expression patterns.**
- "Free returns within 100 days" badge on a tightly cropped product shot.
- Founder voiceover: "If it doesn't work for you, send it back. Free."
- Comparison card: "Most competitors: 30-day returns. We do 100."
- B2B: "Talk to us. If we can't help, we'll send you to the company that can."

**KPIs to expect movement on.** CVR or CPL (primary). Secondary: AOV (e-com, via larger basket confidence), refund or churn rate (watch carefully — should not spike disproportionately).

### 2. Authority Framing

**Mechanism.** Borrow conviction from credible sources outside the brand: experts, certifications, third-party testing, named press, industry awards, customers with credentials, recognised institutions.

**Academic grounding.** *Authority Principle* (Cialdini). Milgram's obedience studies (1963) showed how strongly people defer to perceived expertise. In purchase decisions, authority shortcuts evaluation when the buyer cannot easily judge quality themselves.

**When it works hardest.**
- Categories where quality is hard to verify pre-purchase (technical products, financial services, healthcare-adjacent).
- B2B decisions where the buyer needs internal cover ("we picked the vendor used by [respected peer]").
- MOF / evaluation stage, where the buyer is comparing.
- Audiences with low domain expertise.

**When it underperforms.**
- Highly self-expressive categories where the *audience* is the authority, not external experts.
- When the source feels bought (a paid celebrity who clearly does not use the product).
- B2C audiences who suspect "expert" endorsements are paid placements.

**Expression by vertical.**
- **E-commerce.** "Tested by [credible institution]" stamp; dermatologist on camera (skincare); engineer explaining the product (technical apparel).
- **D2C subscription.** Named nutritionists, doctors, or category experts on the product.
- **B2B SaaS.** Analyst recognition (Gartner, Forrester), named customer logos at the right tier, named consultants who recommend the product.
- **B2C lead gen.** Industry certifications, regulator approvals, named institutional partnerships.

**KPIs.** CVR or CPL, time on landing page, return rate (typically improves).

### 3. Transparency

**Mechanism.** Explain candidly what is true — including unflattering trade-offs. Show cost breakdowns, supplier locations, what the product *isn't* good for, why one choice was made over another.

**Academic grounding.** *Pratfall Effect* (Aronson, Willerman, Floyd, 1966) — competent communicators become *more* trusted when they admit small flaws. Also: in low-trust media environments, transparency functions as a signal that the brand has nothing to hide.

**When it works hardest.**
- Nordic markets specifically. Cultural baseline of trust + scepticism toward marketing. Transparency aligns with the cultural script.
- Sustainability and ethics-driven categories.
- Founder-led brands where the founder's voice carries.
- B2B where the audience suspects vendor spin.
- MOF, especially against a competitor known for vague claims.

**When it underperforms.**
- When transparency is performed rather than real (audiences detect this fast).
- When the trade-offs revealed are damaging without context.

**Expression by vertical.**
- **E-commerce.** Cost breakdown infographics ("Here's what €120 actually pays for"), behind-the-scenes factory footage with named people, founder admitting one limitation.
- **D2C.** Ingredient deep-dives, manufacturing detail, churn data shown openly.
- **B2B SaaS.** Public pricing where the category hides it, "here's what we're not good at" content, honest case studies with the misses.
- **B2C lead gen.** Disclosure of how leads are handled, named partners, plain-language explanations of typical pitfalls in the category.

**KPIs.** Brand search lift, retention, organic share rate, CVR or CPL on warmer audiences. Transparency rarely wins on cold prospecting CTR — its job is conviction, not capture.

## Cluster 2 — Motivation & Identity

**The job:** make the purchase feel meaningful — connected to who the buyer is or wants to become.

In B2C, this cluster connects the product to who the buyer is to themselves and to others. In B2B, it connects the purchase to the buyer's professional identity, career trajectory, or team aspirations.

### 4. Identity Signaling

**Mechanism.** The product or solution expresses who the buyer already is to themselves and to others. The ad shows the buyer's tribe — and the buyer recognises it as theirs.

**Academic grounding.** *Self-Determination Theory* (Deci & Ryan, 1985), specifically the relatedness need. *Symbolic consumption* literature (Belk, 1988) — possessions extend the self. Berger & Heath (2007) on signalling and divergence: people use products to communicate identity especially in domains where identity is contested.

**When it works hardest.**
- Categories with strong subcultural meaning (apparel, eyewear, hobby equipment, indie software).
- B2B where the buyer's professional identity is at stake ("we're a modern data team — we use tools like X").
- Audiences with formed identity (mid-20s and up, professionally established).
- Prospecting stage — identity-led ads disqualify mismatches as fast as they qualify fits.

**When it underperforms.**
- Mass-market categories where forced identity feels exclusionary without commercial upside.
- When the identity portrayed is a cliché the audience has already rejected.

**Expression by vertical.**
- **E-commerce.** Documentary-style portraiture of real customers in their own settings; subcultural artefact mood films; "for the [specific identity descriptor]" copy.
- **B2B SaaS.** Customer logos at the right tier (you are "a Notion company" or "a Linear company"); content that signals what kind of team uses this; community-driven aesthetics.
- **D2C subscription.** Membership framing, named community members, identity-coded brand colours and typography.
- **B2C lead gen.** Imagery that codes the buyer's situation ("homeowners in 35–55 brackets" vs "young professionals renting") through location, props, casting.

**KPIs.** Brand search lift, CTR among target identity segments, follow rate, qualified-traffic share, cost per add-to-cart or qualified MQL. Be careful not to over-optimise on raw CTR — identity ads should *segment*, not maximise reach.

### 5. Aspiration

**Mechanism.** The product represents a *future self* the buyer wants to become. Distinct from Identity Signaling, which is about who the buyer *already is*.

**Academic grounding.** *Possible Selves* theory (Markus & Nurius, 1986). Self-discrepancy theory (Higgins, 1987) — the gap between actual and ideal self motivates behaviour. Aspirational marketing does not sell the gap, it sells the bridge.

**When it works hardest.**
- Lifestyle categories (interiors, wellness, travel, food, home).
- Education and self-improvement.
- B2B career-development categories (certifications, leadership tools, productivity systems).
- Younger audiences forming taste.

**When it underperforms.**
- When the aspirational image is too distant — the gap feels uncrossable, the ad inspires retreat rather than purchase.
- In categories where buyers are confident in their own taste (older, wealthier customers in heritage categories).

**Expression by vertical.**
- **E-commerce / D2C.** The product positioned within a *life* (the dinner, the trip, the apartment), not on its own.
- **Education / courses.** "The career you've been imagining" framed against a credible bridge.
- **B2B SaaS.** "The team you want to be" — efficient, in-control, sought after.
- **B2C lead gen** (home services, financial planning). "The home / future you've been planning."

**KPIs.** Brand search lift, retention, contribution margin (aspirational creative tends to lift AOV in e-com and lead quality in lead gen).

### 6. Commitment & Consistency

**Mechanism.** Get the buyer to take a small step that aligns with the identity they want, then make the larger commitment consistent with that step. Quizzes, profile builders, configurators, calculators, save-for-later, wishlist.

**Academic grounding.** *Commitment & Consistency* (Cialdini). Festinger's cognitive dissonance theory (1957) — people work to align their actions with their stated beliefs.

**When it works hardest.**
- Considered-purchase categories.
- Brands with onboarding-style flows (quiz → personalised result → purchase).
- B2B where the small step is a self-assessment, ROI calculator, or maturity model.
- Email and retargeting layered on top of paid acquisition.

**When it underperforms.**
- When the small step feels like work without value.
- In impulse categories — adds friction where there should be none.

**Expression by vertical.**
- **E-commerce.** "Find your fit" quiz; "save your favourites" prompt; personalised retargeting with the specific items engaged.
- **D2C subscription.** Onboarding quiz that produces a custom plan.
- **B2B SaaS.** ROI calculator, "what's your stack?" diagnostic, maturity assessment.
- **B2C lead gen.** "Get your custom quote" form; "what's your home worth?" calculator.

**KPIs.** CVR or CPL (especially on retargeting), AOV or deal size, repeat purchase or expansion rate, list growth.

## Cluster 3 — Social & Proof Mechanics

**The job:** reduce uncertainty by showing what other people — ideally similar people — already concluded.

The buyer's question is "is this real?" The proof cluster answers it without the brand having to make the claim itself.

### 7. Volume Social Proof

**Mechanism.** Numbers. "10,000 customers." "4.8 stars from 12,400 reviews." "Used by 400+ teams." "Trusted by 2 million homeowners."

**Academic grounding.** *Informational Social Influence* (Sherif, 1936; Asch, 1955) — we infer correctness from consensus, especially under uncertainty. Cialdini's *Social Proof* principle.

**When it works hardest.**
- Mass-market or near-mass categories.
- New brand entrants who have crossed a credibility threshold.
- B2B SaaS once a meaningful customer count exists.
- BOF, layered with risk reversal ("12,000 customers, 100-day returns").

**When it underperforms.**
- Premium / exclusive positioning — large numbers can erode exclusivity.
- Tiny numbers (under ~500 in B2C, under ~50 well-known logos in B2B) often hurt more than they help; better silent than thin.
- When the source is unverifiable.

**Expression by vertical.**
- **E-commerce.** Star ratings, review counts, counter animations, "X bought in the last 24 hours" overlays (sparingly).
- **D2C subscription.** "Active members" count, named community size.
- **B2B SaaS.** Customer logo walls (tier-appropriate), "used by X% of teams in [category]" claims if substantiated.
- **B2C lead gen.** "We've helped X families" anchors.

**KPIs.** CVR or CPL on cold and BOF audiences. Drops cost per result on prospecting when paired with a strong hook.

### 8. Similarity Proof

**Mechanism.** "People like you use this." More targeted than Volume Proof — the customer being shown does not just exist in number, they *resemble the viewer*.

**Academic grounding.** *Similarity-Attraction Effect* (Byrne, 1971). We trust and emulate people we perceive as like ourselves.

**When it works hardest.**
- Niche audiences, subcultures, professions.
- Categories where lifestyle context matters more than product specs.
- B2B where role specificity matters ("how a Head of Growth at a 100-person SaaS uses this").
- Brand-building stage where the ICP is being defined for prospects.

**When it underperforms.**
- When the "like you" portrayed is too generic.
- When the resemblance is forced (hired models pretending to be the audience).

**Expression by vertical.**
- **E-commerce / D2C.** UGC from named customers in their own setting; documentary mini-portraits.
- **B2B SaaS.** Customer stories where the title, team size and tech stack mirror the prospect.
- **B2C lead gen.** "Anna, 42, in Stockholm" testimonials with the specifics that match the targeted segment.

**KPIs.** CTR, CVR or CPL, follow rate on segmented audiences.

### 9. Transformation Proof

**Mechanism.** Before / after. The proof is in the change, made visible.

**Academic grounding.** *Vividness Effect* (Nisbett & Ross, 1980) — concrete, visual, narrative information is more persuasive than abstract claims.

**When it works hardest.**
- Categories where the product's value is *felt* over time: skincare, haircare, fitness, home interiors, education outcomes.
- B2B where the transformation is operational (time saved, error rate down, revenue up).
- BOF and re-engagement of warm audiences.

**When it underperforms.**
- When the transformation is implausible or stylised away from realism (skincare: airbrushed afters destroy credibility; B2B: "we 10x'd revenue" without context).
- In identity-led premium categories where transformation framing feels low-end.

**Expression by vertical.**
- **E-commerce / D2C.** Side-by-side photography under identical lighting; time-stamped progress; outfit transformation reels.
- **B2B SaaS.** Before/after dashboards, time-tracking comparisons, error-rate charts with real numbers.
- **B2C lead gen / services.** Project before/after (renovation, financial planning before/after).

**KPIs.** CVR or CPL, retention, content engagement rate. Strong UGC mining opportunity.

## Cluster 4 — Attention & Cognitive Bias

**The job:** win the first second. None of the other triggers matter if the asset is scrolled past.

Attention is the choke point of paid social. The triggers in this cluster are not better than the others — they are *prerequisite* for the others on cold traffic.

### 10. Loss Aversion

**Mechanism.** Frame the message in terms of what the buyer stands to *lose* by not acting, rather than what they stand to *gain* by acting.

**Academic grounding.** Prospect Theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). Empirically: loss frames typically outperform gain frames by 1.5–2× across experimental settings, with strong effects in health, finance, and time-bounded decisions.

**When it works hardest.**
- Time-bounded offers (drop windows, sale ends, last in stock, pricing tier closing).
- Switching decisions (mortgage, telco, insurance, software contracts coming up for renewal).
- Categories where the buyer has been burned by inaction before.

**When it underperforms.**
- When the "loss" is fabricated and the audience can tell.
- In low-stakes categories where the loss frame feels disproportionate.
- For premium / executive B2B brands: heavy loss-aversion can read as desperate.

**Boundary test for regulated / YMYL categories.** Where worry is the strongest driver but fear-mongering is prohibited (by brand, platform policy, or law), a message that touches the line is acceptable only if it passes all three: (1) **Source** — the claim is verifiable against an authority or a lab. (2) **The home, not the body** — point at what can be tested, never at symptoms in the person (generalise per category: "the product, not the patient"). (3) **Next step in the same breath** — the worry is always followed by the solution. Cross-reference with `05_nordic_market_psychology.md` for market-specific sensitivity.

**Expression by vertical.**
- **E-commerce.** "Last 12 in stock" overlay; countdown timers in email and on-site.
- **D2C.** Drop windows, capped membership ("we close registration on Friday").
- **B2B SaaS.** Pricing tier closing, end-of-quarter discount with a real reason, contract renewal-window framing.
- **B2C lead gen.** Interest rate environments, tax-year deadlines, regulatory deadlines.

**KPIs.** CTR (sharp lift), CVR or CPL (modest lift), AOV or deal size (often flat or slightly down — buyers complete fast and small).

### 11. Curiosity / Open Loop

**Mechanism.** Open an information gap that the viewer needs to close. Withhold the answer until after the click or the next beat of the video.

**Academic grounding.** *Information Gap Theory of Curiosity* (Loewenstein, 1994) — curiosity is the aversive experience of an unresolved gap; people are motivated to close it.

**When it works hardest.**
- Hook stage of video creative (first 1.5 seconds).
- New / unfamiliar product categories.
- Editorial-style and pattern-interrupt content.
- B2B "you're doing X — here's the smarter way" hooks.

**When it underperforms.**
- When the resolution is anticlimactic (clickbait penalty in algorithms and audience trust).
- In BOF — the buyer wants information density, not teasing.

**Expression by vertical.**
- **E-commerce.** "We almost didn't launch this. Here's why we did." Visual gap.
- **D2C.** "I tried [thing] for 30 days. Here's what happened." Founder admissions.
- **B2B SaaS.** "Why we stopped doing X" content; provocative reframings.
- **B2C lead gen.** "The thing your [advisor/broker/insurer] won't tell you."

**KPIs.** CTR, hook rate (3-second view rate), thumb-stop rate. Watch CVR or CPL carefully — curiosity ads often have a CTR/CVR mismatch.

### 12. Contrast & Anchoring

**Mechanism.** Position the product against a reference point that makes it look like the obvious choice. Side-by-side comparison, decoy options, before/after price reveals.

**Academic grounding.** *Anchoring & Adjustment* (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974) — initial reference points distort all subsequent judgements. *Decoy Effect* (Huber, Payne, Puto, 1982) — adding an asymmetrically dominated option shifts preference.

**When it works hardest.**
- Categories with a clear competitor to anchor against.
- Multi-tier pricing (compare-the-plans creative).
- BOF, especially against price objections.

**When it underperforms.**
- When the comparison flatters the competitor.
- When the anchor is so high it loses credibility.

**Expression by vertical.**
- **E-commerce.** Split-screen with the competitor named or implied; three-tier price card with a clearly intended winner.
- **B2B SaaS.** Feature comparison tables, "the cost of doing nothing" framings, ROI calculators that anchor against status quo.
- **B2C lead gen.** "What you'd usually pay vs what you pay here" reveal; comparison against provider categories ("vs banks").

**KPIs.** CVR or CPL, AOV or deal size (anchoring tends to lift basket size or contract tier).

## Cluster 5 — Urgency & Value

**The job:** make acting *now* rational, and make what the buyer already holds feel worth keeping.

Where clusters 1–4 build the case for buying at all, this cluster times the decision and prices it emotionally. It is the cluster most often faked — and faked urgency or faked generosity damages trust faster than any other creative mistake, especially on Nordic audiences (see `05_nordic_market_psychology.md`).

### 13. Scarcity / Urgency

**Mechanism.** Limit availability in units or in time, and say so plainly. A real constraint converts deliberation into decision.

**Academic grounding.** Scarcity principle (Cialdini, *Influence*, 1984); reactance theory — restricted options gain perceived value. Closely coupled to Loss Aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979): scarcity is the *situation*, loss aversion the *feeling* it produces.

**When it works hardest.**
- BOF / conversion — the buyer already wants it; the constraint answers "why now?".
- Drops, seasonal collections, genuinely limited runs, deadline-bound offers (contract dates, enrollment windows).
- Retargeting on engaged non-buyers ("your size is almost gone").

**When it underperforms.**
- Manufactured scarcity — countdown timers that reset, "only 3 left" on inventory that never runs out. On Nordic markets this reads as manipulation and burns the brand, not just the ad.
- TOF on cold audiences with no established desire — scarcity of a thing nobody wants yet is noise.

**Creative expression patterns.**
- "Last sizes" overlay on the product shot; "Drop closes Sunday" endcard.
- Restock announcement framed as scarcity resolved ("Back — for now").
- B2B: cohort or onboarding-window deadlines ("Q3 onboarding closes June 15").

**KPIs to expect movement on.** CVR, cost per purchase, time-to-conversion (primary). Watch refund rate — a spike signals pressured, regretted purchases.

### 14. Reciprocity

**Mechanism.** Give real value first — knowledge, a tool, a sample — and the buyer feels a pull to give back. The ad is generous before it asks.

**Academic grounding.** Reciprocity norm (Gouldner, 1960; Cialdini, *Influence*, 1984). The effect scales with the perceived value and unexpectedness of what is given, not with what is asked.

**When it works hardest.**
- TOF / MOF — value-first content earns the attention and the trust the later stages spend.
- Lead gen: guides, calculators, checklists, free audits with no strings visible.
- Categories where the buyer needs education before they can want the product.

**When it underperforms.**
- BOF — the buyer is deciding, not collecting gifts; generosity here delays instead of closes.
- When the "gift" is thinly veiled sales material — the pull reverses into resistance.

**Creative expression patterns.**
- "The 5 mistakes everyone makes with X" carousel that actually teaches.
- Free tool or quiz whose result is useful even without buying.
- Founder sharing the recipe/method openly ("here's how to do it yourself — or we'll do it for you").

**KPIs to expect movement on.** CTR, lead volume and CPL (primary). Secondary: lead-to-MQL rate — reciprocity done right attracts qualified attention, done wrong it attracts freebie hunters.

### 15. Endowment Effect

**Mechanism.** Make the buyer feel they already own it — then not buying feels like giving something up. Ownership is simulated before purchase.

**Academic grounding.** Endowment Effect (Thaler, 1980; Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, 1990) — people value what they hold above what they could acquire. Trials, carts and personalised results create *psychological* holding.

**When it works hardest.**
- Retargeting / BOF — the wishlist, the cart, the quiz result, the configured product, the trial in progress: all are things the buyer now "has".
- Try-before-you-buy mechanics, home try-on, free trials with saved state.
- Personalisation that produces an artifact ("your plan", "your shade", "your size profile").

**When it underperforms.**
- Cold TOF — there is nothing owned yet to protect.
- When the simulated ownership is generic ("people like you chose...") rather than *this buyer's* artifact.

**Creative expression patterns.**
- Dynamic retargeting that shows *the buyer's* items, framed as theirs ("Your cart is waiting — sizes reserved 24h").
- Trial-expiry creative centred on what will be lost, not what could be gained.
- "Your results" framing on quiz and configurator follow-ups.

**KPIs to expect movement on.** CVR on retargeting audiences, cart recovery rate, trial-to-paid rate (primary). Secondary: frequency tolerance — endowment framing sustains more impressions before fatigue than generic retargeting.

## Layering and primacy

Most strong creative carries 1–2 triggers, with one clearly primary. Common productive combinations:

- **Loss Aversion + Volume Proof** ("Last 200 — 4.8★, 12,000 customers")
- **Identity Signaling + Similarity Proof** (documentary portrait of a real customer)
- **Authority + Transparency** (founder explaining the formulation candidly)
- **Curiosity + Transformation Proof** (open loop hook → before/after reveal)
- **Aspiration + Commitment & Consistency** (hero film → quiz / calculator CTA)
- **Authority + Risk Reversal** (B2B: named experts + money-back guarantee)

Avoid stacking three or more triggers — the message becomes unreadable in the first second.

## Framing modifier: EGC (Employee-Generated Content)

EGC is creative where the spokesperson is an **employee of the brand** — an in-house expert, craftsperson, store staff, or founder. It is not a sixteenth trigger. It is a distinct **delivery modality** of two existing triggers: it activates **Authority Framing** (through category expertise) and **Social & Proof Mechanics** (the person uses and evaluates the product on camera) — but structurally differently from external creator content (IGC) or anonymous customer content (UGC).

EGC works hardest in categories where:

- The category has inherent complexity that demands expertise (fragrance, wine, finance, skincare, footwear).
- The buyer's journey is long and information-seeking.
- Brand equity is tied to competence rather than volume.

Operational differences vs UGC and IGC: EGC is scalable over time (the same spokesperson compounds over years), brand-controlled in rights and tone, cost-fixed (salaries, not per-production fees), and carries inherently lower brand-conflict risk than external creators.

**In audits and Ads Library passes**, classify creative three ways — partnership/creator ads, EGC, brand ads — not two. "0 % partnership, 0 % EGC in paid despite strong EGC in organic, 100 % brand ads" is a far more useful signal than "0 % partnership / 100 % brand". See M8/M9 in the Creative audit method.

## What is *not* a trigger

- **A discount.** A discount is a *mechanic*. It can be expressed through Loss Aversion, Contrast & Anchoring, or Risk Reversal — but it is not itself a psychological trigger.
- **A celebrity or named expert.** A celebrity can carry Authority, Identity Signaling, or Similarity Proof — depending on who they are to the audience. The trigger is the relationship, not the person.
- **A trend or aesthetic.** "Y2K" or "brutalist B2B web design" is not a trigger. It can be the *expression* of an Identity Signaling or Aspiration trigger, but only if the underlying audience-identity work has been done.
